\section*{B1 (concept)}

\begin{description}
\item[$Q_1$] Complete, not sound: $(1, 5)$ meets $y \ge 0$, and the reference
  gives $1$.
\item[$Q_2$] Complete, not sound: $(1, 5)$ again ($5 \ge 0$ and $5 \ge 1$).
  Adding a conjunct made it stronger and still not sound.
\item[$Q_3$] Complete, not sound: $(-3, -3)$ meets $y = x$, and the reference
  gives $0$.
\item[$Q_4$] Sound and complete: it is the reference split by cases.
\item[$Q_5$] Neither: $(-1, -1)$ is allowed and wrong (not sound); $(-1, 0)$ is
  the reference output and not allowed (not complete).
\item[(c)] A client prefers a sound postcondition (everything it may conclude
  is true of the real result; $Q_4$ tells it the most); an implementer finds a
  weak, complete one easiest ($Q_1$ holds of the reference with almost no
  proof). Strength helps the caller and costs the callee, so a specification
  is judged from both sides (Q10's spec items; the client checks of H3v).
  Note: completeness here is relative to the reference outputs; an
  incomplete postcondition makes the correct implementation fail to verify.
\end{description}
Any pairs that show the same failures are correct.
