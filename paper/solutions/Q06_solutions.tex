\section*{How these are graded}
B1, B2 and B4 are hand calculations, dimension \texttt{recomputation}: the
grader fills the rubric's statistics probe (Probes A, B and D) with the
learner's numbers and reads, step by step, where an error entered (the
probe's \emph{carried} column recomputes each step from the learner's own
earlier values, its \emph{fresh} column from the problem's numbers). B3 is a
concept problem, dimension \texttt{correctness}, with its counts checked by
Probe C. B5 is a paper card (\texttt{card-fidelity}, per field). The solutions
below give the answers; the rubric never states them. The pair holds no
blueprint, so \texttt{/grade-paper Q06 --post} has nothing to re-score.

\section*{B1 (hand-calculation): 17 of 20}

\textbf{(a)} With $\hat p = 17/20 = 0.85$, $n = 20$, $z = 1.96$, $z^2 = 3.8416$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $z^2/n = 0.19208$; the denominator is $1 + z^2/n = 1.19208$;
\item centre $= (\hat p + z^2/(2n)) / (1 + z^2/n) = (0.85 + 0.09604)/1.19208
  = 0.94604/1.19208 = 0.7936$;
\item under the root: $\hat p(1-\hat p)/n + z^2/(4n^2) = 0.1275/20 + 3.8416/1600
  = 0.006375 + 0.002401 = 0.008776$, whose root is $0.09368$;
\item half-width $= 1.96 \times 0.09368 / 1.19208 = 0.18361/1.19208 = 0.1540$;
\item interval $(0.7936 - 0.1540,\ 0.7936 + 0.1540) = (0.640,\ 0.948)$, width
  $0.308$.
\end{itemize}
\texttt{fvh.stats.wilson\_interval(17, 20)} prints
\texttt{(0.6395811352592431, 0.9476312541037835)}: it uses the exact normal
quantile ($1.95996\ldots$), which agrees with $z = 1.96$ to the third decimal.

\textbf{(b)} Wald: $0.85 \pm 1.96\sqrt{0.006375} = 0.85 \pm 1.96 \times 0.07984 =
0.85 \pm 0.1565$, so $(0.694,\ 1.006)$. Its upper end exceeds $1$: an interval
for a proportion must lie in $[0, 1]$. Its shape: Wald is symmetric about
$\hat p$; Wilson's centre ($0.794$) is pulled from $\hat p$ toward $1/2$, and
the interval is asymmetric about $\hat p$ ($0.210$ below, $0.098$ above). Either
difference (symmetry, or the centre not at $\hat p$) is a full answer; a third
correct one is that Wald's width goes to zero as $\hat p \to 1$, Wilson's does
not.

\textbf{(c)} About $4\times$, so $n \approx 80$: the half-width is of order
$z\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$, which scales as $1/\sqrt{n}$ (the $z^2/n$ terms
shrink too and change little). Recomputed: the width $0.3081$ at $17/20$
becomes $0.1562$ at $68/80$, ratio $0.507$.

\textbf{Probe A on the pins} (the gold fixture's numbers): every step
\texttt{ok}, exit 0, and the (c) line
\texttt{width at n = 20: 0.3081; at n = 80 (68 successes): 0.1562; ratio 0.507}.

\textbf{Typical flaws, with their instances.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The half-width not divided by $1 + z^2/n$ ($0.184$; interval
  $(0.610, 0.978)$): Probe A marks \texttt{half\_width} \texttt{ERROR ENTERS
  HERE} (yours $0.18400$, carried $0.15407$), and \texttt{lo}, \texttt{hi}
  \texttt{inherited}. This is the flawed fixture's B1.
\item The centre left at $\hat p$ (a Wald centre with a Wilson half-width):
  \texttt{centre} enters, with carried $0.79362$.
\item $z^2/(4n^2)$ written as $z^2/(4n)$ ($0.048$): \texttt{under\_root}
  enters.
\item (b) answered by width alone (``Wald is narrower, so it overstates
  precision''): here it is slightly wider ($0.313$ against $0.308$); the
  recomputed Wald bounds are the instance, and the property it breaks is the
  range $[0, 1]$.
\end{itemize}

\section*{B2 (hand-calculation): pass@2 on three tasks}

\textbf{(a)} $n = 5$, $k = 2$, $\binom{5}{2} = 10$.
\begin{itemize}
\item t1, $c = 0$: $1 - \binom{5}{2}/\binom{5}{2} = 1 - 10/10 = 0$;
\item t2, $c = 2$: $1 - \binom{3}{2}/\binom{5}{2} = 1 - 3/10 = 0.7$;
\item t3, $c = 4$: $n - c = 1 < k$, so $1$ (equivalently $\binom{1}{2} = 0$).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{(b)} pass@2 $= (0 + 0.7 + 1)/3 = 0.567$; pass@1 is $c/n$ per task,
$(0 + 0.4 + 0.8)/3 = 0.4$.

\textbf{(c)} Plug-in for t2: $1 - (1 - 0.4)^2 = 1 - 0.36 = 0.64$, below
$0.7$. The plug-in treats the $k$ attempts as independent draws, with
replacement, from a known success rate $c/n$; the definition counts the
$k$-subsets of the $n$ observed samples, drawn without replacement, that hold
no success. Either half (with replacement, or the rate treated as known) is a
full answer.

\textbf{Probe B on the pins:} \texttt{fvh.stats.pass\_at\_k} gives
\texttt{\{'t1': 0.0, 't2': 0.7, 't3': 1.0\}} and pass@1
\texttt{\{'t1': 0.0, 't2': 0.4, 't3': 0.8\}}.

\textbf{Typical flaws.}
\begin{itemize}
\item $\binom{c}{k}$ in place of $\binom{n - c}{k}$ for t2:
  $1 - \binom{2}{2}/10 = 0.9$. Probe B marks \texttt{t2} \texttt{ERROR ENTERS
  HERE} (yours $0.9$, carried $0.7$, binomials \texttt{C(3, 2) = 3, C(5, 2) =
  10}) and the mean $0.633$ \texttt{inherited}. This is the flawed fixture's
  B2.
\item t3 computed with $\binom{1}{2}$ taken as $1$ (giving $0.9$): the
  problem fixes $\binom{a}{b} = 0$ for $b > a$; t3 enters.
\item The benchmark's pass@2 computed from the pooled counts
  ($c = 6$ of $15$): it is the mean of per-task estimates; \texttt{pass2}
  enters against the learner's own per-task values.
\end{itemize}

\section*{B3 (concept): why first attempts}

\textbf{(a)} Pass rate $1/4 = 0.25$ (only t2's first attempt succeeds);
successes among records $3/9 = 0.333$; tasks solved at some attempt
$3/4 = 0.75$.

\textbf{(b)} The pass rate estimates the chance that one fresh attempt on a
task like these succeeds, with the task as the unit: one record per task, so
the $n$ of the interval is the number of tasks. The other two measure other
things. The share of records depends on the retry policy: a task contributes
more records the more it fails, so hard tasks are over-weighted, and changing
the cap changes the number without changing the prover. The share of tasks
solved within the cap is a success-within-a-budget metric (three attempts,
each after a failure), not a single-attempt rate. Counting every record treats
the retries of one task as independent samples; they are not (same task, and
a retry happens only after a failure). It sets $n = 9$ instead of $4$, which
narrows the interval with no new information about new tasks.

\textbf{(c)} The attempt budget (three), whether the attempts are independent
samples or retries that see earlier failures (feedback), the number of tasks,
and what counts as success (verified, zero holes). Without these it compares
with neither a first-attempt rate nor a pass@$k$ from independent samples.

\textbf{Probe C on the pins:} \texttt{tasks 4, records 9}; the three numbers
above.

\textbf{Typical flaws.} Taking $3/9$ as the pass rate (``every attempt is a
sample''); taking $3/4$ as the pass rate; (b) arguing only ``it is the
convention'' (a gap, 3: no mechanism named); (c) naming only the task count.
B3 is the flawed fixture's clean control.

\section*{B4 (hand-calculation): a paired comparison}

\textbf{(a)} A: $(14 + 1)/30 = 0.5$; B: $(14 + 10)/30 = 0.8$. Only the
discordant tasks carry information on which prover is better: $b = 1$ (A only),
$c = 10$ (B only), $11$ tasks. The $14$ both solve and the $5$ both miss say
nothing about the difference.

\textbf{(b)} $X \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(11, 1/2)$; $\min(b, c) = 1$;
$P(X \leq 1) = \bigl(\binom{11}{0} + \binom{11}{1}\bigr)/2^{11} = (1 + 11)/2048
= 12/2048$; $p = 2 \times 12/2048 = 24/2048 = 0.0117 < 0.05$: reject that the
two provers are equally likely to win a discordant task; B solves more.
\texttt{fvh.stats.mcnemar(1, 10)} prints \texttt{0.01171875}.

\textbf{(c)} With $140$ concordant successes the rates become $141/156 = 0.904$
and $150/156 = 0.962$: the gap shrinks from $0.30$ to $0.058$. $b$, $c$ and $p$
do not change: concordant tasks never enter the test.

\textbf{Probe D on the pins} (the gold fixture's numbers): every step
\texttt{ok}, exit 0; the (c) line
\texttt{(c) with 140 both: rates 0.9038 and 0.9615; fvh.stats.mcnemar(1, 10) =
0.01171875}.

\textbf{Typical flaws.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The factor $2$ dropped (a one-sided $p = 12/2048 = 0.0059$): Probe D marks
  \texttt{p} \texttt{ERROR ENTERS HERE} (yours $0.00586$, carried $0.01172$).
  This is the flawed fixture's B4; the decision happens to survive, the value
  does not.
\item $b + c$ taken as $30$ (all tasks): $p$ enters against the learner's own
  $b$ and $c$.
\item (c) answered ``the p-value grows, since the rates are closer'': the
  probe's (c) line shows the same $0.01171875$.
\end{itemize}
