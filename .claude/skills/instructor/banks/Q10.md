### K1 [predict] — a byte, two ways
**viva:** yes (verifier-refereed predict) · **strand:** S1 · **tags:** `bit-vector`, `exec-overflow`, `false-friends` · **backward-edge:** Q07 (bit-vectors and words)

Dafny: two methods convert a `nat` to a bit-vector, `ToByte` to `bv8` under `requires x < 256` and `ToWord32` to `bv32` under `requires x < 4294967296`, each with `ensures b as nat == x`. Verus: `to_byte` casts a `u16` below 256 to `u8` under `ensures b == x`, and `to_byte_unchecked` does the same cast with no `requires`. Predict `scripts/vcheck --module Q10`'s verdict on each (`D:<ToByte> <ToWord32> V:<to_byte> <to_byte_unchecked>`, each `ok`, `fail` or `timeout`). Then: does the width matter in Dafny, and what does Verus's `as` do to a value that does not fit?

**Target insights**
- In Dafny, the round trip `b as nat == x` relates a bit-vector to an integer, and at the course's resource limit neither width closes: both methods run out of resource. The width is not the problem; the bridge between the two theories is. (P1dv is the same lesson at 64 bits.)
- The cast itself is cheap: its range check is the `requires`. The BigNat spine keeps limbs as `nat` in Dafny for this reason; bit-vectors are for bit-level facts, inside proofs.
- In Verus, `u8`, `u16` and `u64` are integers to the solver, bounded by their types: `to_byte`'s claim is linear arithmetic and verifies.
- Verus's `as` between integer types truncates (Rust's semantics, modelled in the spec), so without the `requires` the postcondition is false for `x >= 256`, and the verifier says so. A Dafny cast to a `bv` type is checked instead: the range check would fail at the call.

**Misconceptions & counter-moves**
- *"A smaller bit-vector is easier, so `bv8` verifies."* → Probe: both Dafny methods; ask: what is the solver asked to relate, and does the width change that?
- *"Verus's cast panics when the value does not fit, so `to_byte_unchecked` is safe."* → Probe: the Verus file; ask: what does `x as u8` compute for `x == 300`?
- *"Dafny and Verus treat a cast the same way."* → Ask: which one checks the range at the cast, and which one defines the result?

**Verifier probe**

### K2 [why] — a statement is all a caller knows
**viva:** yes · **strand:** S2 · **tags:** `requires-ensures`, `spec-strength` · **backward-edge:** Q01 (the contract: a caller knows only the `ensures`)

`TwoCopies(x)` returns `x + x`, and its `ensures` says only `r >= x`. A client calls it twice and claims the result is `4 * x` (Verus: once, claiming `2 * x`). The client fails, although the body of `TwoCopies` computes exactly what the client needs. Why does the verifier not use the body, and what is the smallest change that makes the client verify?

**Target insights**
- Verification is modular: a call is checked against the callee's `requires` and replaced by its `ensures`. The body is checked once, against its own statement, and never again at a call. A statement too weak for a client fails the client, wherever the body's truth lies.
- The fix belongs in the callee's statement (`r == x + x`, Verus `r == 2 * x`), and then both the body and the client verify. Changing the client, or inlining the body, would hide the weak statement from every other caller.
- This is how the module's spec items are judged (H2d, H2v): the client is the reader that decides whether a statement says enough.
- Modularity is why a verified library is reusable: a caller depends on a statement, not on an implementation that may change.

**Misconceptions & counter-moves**
- *"The verifier should see that `x + x == 2 * x`."* → Ask: where in the client does `x + x` appear? (Only in `TwoCopies`'s body.)
- *"Add an `assert` in the client."* → Ask: what fact could that assert use about the call's result, other than the `ensures`?
- *"Make the client's claim weaker."* → Ask: whose statement is wrong, the client's or the callee's?

**Verifier probe**

### K3 [predict] — where the error lands
**viva:** yes (verifier-refereed predict) · **strand:** S3 · **tags:** `verdict-reading`, `assertion-repair` · **backward-edge:** Q03 (what a failure message does and does not say)

`Treads(n)` adds 3 for each step down to 0. A lemma claims `Treads(n) == 3 * n`, with Dafny's automatic induction switched off, and its body asserts one unfolding, `Treads(n) == 3 + Treads(n - 1)`, in the case `n > 0`, and nothing else. Predict, in each language, which line `scripts/vcheck --module Q10` reports: the assertion, the postcondition, or both (`D:<line> V:<line>`, each `assert`, `post` or `both`). Then: what is missing, and why is it not reported where it is missing?

**Target insights**
- The assertion holds: it is one unfolding of the definition, which both verifiers do on their own. The postcondition fails: from the unfolding alone, `Treads(n - 1)` is an unknown number.
- The missing fact is the induction hypothesis, the lemma at `n - 1`, and the repair is one call. Neither verifier names it: a failure is reported where a fact is first *needed* (the postcondition on that return path), never where it is *missing*.
- So a failure message gives a *where* (the obligation and its path) and never a *what*. The card of this module (02.11, Laurel) builds its placeholder on exactly that split.
- With the call added, both files verify.

**Misconceptions & counter-moves**
- *"The assertion fails, because the solver cannot unfold."* → Probe: the files; ask: which line has the error?
- *"The error line is where to add the fix."* → Ask: which fact would make the postcondition follow, and where does that fact come from?
- *"Dafny proves it anyway by induction."* → Ask: what does `{:induction false}` switch off, and why does the probe use it?

### K4 [reconstruct] — the invariants of a maximum
**viva:** yes · **strand:** S4 · **tags:** `loop-invariant` · **backward-edge:** Q02 (loops: an invariant is what the loop keeps)

`Largest(s)` scans a non-empty sequence and returns its maximum: every element is at most `m`, and some element equals `m`. Reconstruct the loop's invariants from its postconditions, in both languages, and say what fails if the one about "some element equals `m`" is left out.

**Target insights**
- Each postcondition becomes an invariant with `i` in place of `|s|`: every element below `i` is at most `m`; some element below `i` equals `m`. Plus the bounds `1 <= i <= |s|`, which make the substitution exact at the exit.
- The initial state satisfies them because `m := s[0]` and `i := 1`; one step keeps them because the new element is either at most `m` or becomes `m`.
- Without the existential invariant the loop still verifies its other clauses, and the postcondition `exists j …` fails: the loop forgot that `m` came from `s`.
- The method is the chapter's §4: the exit, then the substitution, then initialization and preservation.

**Misconceptions & counter-moves**
- *"The invariant is the postcondition."* → Ask: at `i == 1`, does "every element of `s` is at most `m`" hold?
- *"The existential is obvious: `m` is always some `s[j]`."* → Probe: the file without it; ask: what does the verifier know about `m` after the loop?
- *"`i <= |s|` is implied by the guard."* → Ask: at the exit, what does the negated guard give without it?

**Verifier probe**
```dafny
module ProbeK4 {
  method Largest(s: seq<nat>) returns (m: nat)
    requires |s| > 0
    ensures forall j | 0 <= j < |s| :: s[j] <= m
    ensures exists j | 0 <= j < |s| :: s[j] == m
  {
    m := s[0];
    var i := 1;
    while i < |s|
      invariant 1 <= i <= |s|
      invariant forall j | 0 <= j < i :: s[j] <= m
      invariant exists j | 0 <= j < i :: s[j] == m
      decreases |s| - i
    {
      if s[i] > m {
        m := s[i];
      }
      i := i + 1;
    }
  }
}
```

### K5 [predict] — what a loop forgets
**viva:** yes (verifier-refereed predict) · **strand:** S4 · **tags:** `loop-invariant`, `false-friends` · **backward-edge:** none

Before a loop, `n` is set to the length of the sequence (Dafny `var n := |s|;`, Verus `let n = v.len();`), and the loop runs `while i < n` with the invariant `i <= n` (Verus also `c <= i`), reading element `i` in the body. Nothing else is stated. Predict `scripts/vcheck --module Q10`'s verdict in each language (`D:<v> V:<v>`, each `ok` or `fail`). If one fails, which obligation, and what one line fixes it?

**Target insights**
- Dafny keeps every fact about a variable the loop does not assign: `n == |s|` holds in the body, so `s[i]` is in range and the file verifies.
- Verus's loop sees only its invariants (and the types of its variables): the fact `n == v.len()` from before the loop is gone inside it, so `v[i]`'s precondition `i < v.len()` fails. Adding `n == v.len()` to the invariant fixes it.
- The same shape, two context rules: in Verus, write into the invariant every fact about the context that the body needs, even about variables the loop never changes.
- This is also why P2dv's Verus loop without its invariant failed on the increment: nothing bounded `i`.

**Misconceptions & counter-moves**
- *"`n` never changes, so both verifiers know it."* → Probe: both files; ask: what does each verifier assume at the top of the body?
- *"The Verus error is about `i` going past `n`."* → Ask: which precondition is it, and what does it compare `i` with?
- *"Dafny is unsound here."* → Ask: is `n == |s|` true at every iteration? Who proved it?

**Verifier probe**
```dafny
module ProbeK5 {

      decreases n - i
    {
      t := t + s[i];

    }
  }
}
```

### K6 [connect] — a clock face, found by its shape
**viva:** yes · **strand:** S5 · **tags:** `search-then-name`, `spec-wellformedness` · **backward-edge:** Q04 (the search ladder, and a library call's `requires` checked at the call)

Adding on a clock face of `h` hours: reducing each summand first and then the sum gives the same remainder as reducing the sum. The proof is one call of a library lemma, in each language, with nonlinear arithmetic off in the Dafny module. Q04 gave you a search ladder. What would you search for, and what in the hit decides whether the call works here? Then: the statement takes `h: nat`. Both runs fail; what do the errors say, and what is the fix?

**Target insights**
- Search by the shape of the conclusion: a remainder of a sum of remainders, `(x % m + y % m) % m`. The hit is `LemmaAddModNoop` in `Std.Arithmetic.DivMod` and `lemma_add_mod_noop` in `vstd::arithmetic::div_mod`, each with `requires 0 < m`.
